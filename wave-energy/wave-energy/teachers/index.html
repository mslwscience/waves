Wave Energy Lab: Teacher Guide

Free browser activities for grade 7: real NOAA buoy data, a fair-test lab with graphing, a design-test-improve game, integer practice on wave graphs, and two coached CERs. Built for low readers and online classes.

How each part works

🌊 Catch the Wave (about 20–30 min, plus the Wave Lab)

  1. Learn: crest, trough, wave height and time between waves on a labeled diagram. A note sets up the water dot.
  2. Make power: one slider changes wave height. A float bobs, a generator spins, and houses light up. A yellow “water dot” moves in a circle to show waves move energy, not water. Three challenges and two “What did you notice?” questions.
  3. Pick a spot: students test six places and fill a data table, then pick the best spot and finish a sentence starter.
  4. Check: five multiple-choice questions with a first-try score.
  5. Wave Lab: a six-stage fair test modeled on the Virtual Science Teachers CER module: set constants, collect data, plot the graph (tap the grid or use dropdowns), read the graph, build the CER with ? help buttons, and turn in the final report.

⚡ Wave Power Engineer (about 30–45 min per session)

  1. Ask: students compare 12 NOAA buoys (Lake Erie near Cleveland to the Bering Sea) on bar charts. Buoy Detective questions pay $10 each.
  2. Plan: pick a machine type, size and strength under a budget. Live checks warn about storm strength, goal homes and machine fit.
  3. Test: a 48-hour storm test with a live wave-height graph and a dashed “breaks above” line. If a wave crosses it, the machine breaks.
  4. Improve: three stars (survived, met the homes goal, saved $15+), engineer tips, a power report card, the Power Ohio challenge and an Engineer’s Notebook of every test.
  5. Report: the “Could we really power Ohio?” coached CER. It unlocks after one Lake Erie test and one ocean test.

🎯 Buoy Training (10–15 min, good as a warm-up)

Line graphs of water level at a real buoy, with still water = 0. Four levels: read a point, wave height = crest − trough, change = end − start, and mixed (including “Which point is lower?”). Wrong answers are the common sign mistakes, and each one gets its own coaching plus a vertical number line. $5 per first-try answer, up to $50 for the game budget.

🔊 Sound Relay (30–45 min)

Seven design challenges. Students build a path from everyday things, predict, then test. The animation shows a longitudinal wave: particles bunch up (compressions) and spread out (rarefactions), and one red particle only wiggles back and forth while the wave moves on. A loudness bar chart shows how much each part kept, and the race challenges show travel time.

  1. Whisper Across the Room: air, tubes, hose, tin cans on tight vs. loose string.
  2. Knock Through the Wall: solids carry vibration; a cup or your ear on the wall helps; blankets absorb.
  3. Hear It Through Your Fingers: the spoon-on-a-string “bell” (fingertips over the ears, never in them).
  4. Underwater Click: water carries sound well; the water surface reflects it.
  5. Sound Race (340 m): air about 343 m/s, water about 1,480, wood about 3,800, steel rod about 5,100.
  6. Talk in Space: shouting fails (no medium); touching helmets or a tight tether string works; radio and flashlight deliver the message as electromagnetic waves, not sound.
  7. Soundproof the Nursery: the reverse challenge. Soft materials absorb energy (it becomes a tiny bit of heat, never lost).

Key: 3-star designs are tight-string cans; knuckles + plain wall + ear on wall; tight string + fingertips; click and listen underwater; ear on the steel railing; touch helmets (and test shouting once); rug + books + towel. CER: No, a sound wave cannot travel through empty space. Evidence: 0 arrived when shouting; 35 (helmets) or 28 (tether) arrived; the radio message was an electromagnetic wave. Reasoning: sound is a mechanical, longitudinal wave; particles push on the next particles; space has almost no particles.

🕵️ Quake Detective (45–60 min over 4.11–4.12)

  1. Watch P and S waves: two rock strips. In the P-wave the red particle slides back and forth along the path (longitudinal); in the S-wave it moves up and down (transverse). A seismograph shows the P-wave arriving first with small fast wiggles, then the S-wave with bigger ones. Four check questions.
  2. Read a seismogram: three practice graphs. Students tap where the P-wave starts (first wiggles) and the S-wave starts (wiggles jump bigger), subtract to get the S − P gap, then multiply by 8 km. Typed entry works instead of tapping.
  3. Find the real earthquake: Toledo, Columbus and Pittsburgh stations. Each distance draws a circle on an Ohio map; students tap where the circles cross. The reveal is the June 10, 2019 magnitude 4.0 earthquake under Lake Erie just north of Eastlake.
  4. CER: “Why does the P-wave arrive first?” using the student’s own times.

Model note: seismograms are modeled from the real epicenter using P about 6 km/s and S about 3.43 km/s, so every second of S − P gap is 8 km. Depth and rock layers are ignored. Key: practice gaps 3, 6 and 12 s (24, 48, 96 km); Toledo about 21.5 s (172 km), Columbus about 29 s (232 km), Pittsburgh about 23.5 s (187 km). Taps within 1 s count. CER: P travels faster through rock; P is longitudinal (push and pull), S is transverse; the gap grows with distance.

Standards guardrail: why “time between waves” is held at 8 seconds

Ohio’s model curriculum says a wave’s energy increases with amplitude and decreases with increasing wavelength. That second part fits light, but real ocean swells do the opposite: long, slow swells carry more power. If students measured power while changing the time between waves, their data would seem to contradict the standard.

So in every activity: time between waves is locked at 8 seconds, power depends only on wave height (amplitude), and no question asks students to connect wave period or wavelength to energy. Buoy cards show wave height only. Machine “fit” in the game depends on wave height (near-shore flap door for small waves, offshore bobbing buoy for medium and tall waves).

Answer keys

Catch the Wave

  • Step 2 questions: Taller waves make more power. Shorter waves make less power.
  • Challenges: 100 homes needs about 1.3 m or taller. 1,000 homes needs about 3.9 m or taller. Fewer than 5 homes needs the slider at 0.2 m.
  • Step 3: Oregon coast (2.5 m) makes the most power.
  • Check: 1 Energy. 2 A tall wave. 3 It bobs up and down to turn a generator. 4 The waves are small (short). 5 It will not run out.

Wave Lab

Wave heightPower
1.0 m80 kW
2.0 m320 kW
3.0 m720 kW
4.0 m1,280 kW
  • Constants: change wave height; time between waves 8 s and machine width 20 m stay the same.
  • Read the graph: 1 → 2 m: 4 times. 2 → 4 m: 4 times. Shape: a curve that bends upward. 2.5 m: about 500 kW.
  • Exemplar CER. Claim: Making waves taller makes the power go up. When the height doubles, the power becomes about 4 times bigger. Evidence: When the wave height doubled from 1 m to 2 m, the power went from 80 kW to 320 kW. When the height doubled again from 2 m to 4 m, the power went from 320 kW to 1,280 kW. Reasoning: Taller waves carry more energy. When the height doubles, the energy becomes about 4 times bigger. That is why my graph curves upward. A wave machine cannot make new energy. It can only catch the energy the wave carries.

Could we really power Ohio? (Wave Power Engineer)

  • Data question: the ocean place makes more power per machine.
  • Claim: No. Lake Erie waves do not have enough energy to power all of Ohio.
  • Real evidence cards: machines needed and miles of shore; wave heights compared; homes per machine compared; Lake Erie freezes in winter. Traps: “fun place to visit,” “machines look cool,” “Lake Erie has a lot of water.” The coach requires 2+ cards, one with numbers, one that compares.
  • Reasoning: must include “Energy cannot be created or destroyed. A machine can only catch energy the waves already carry.” Supporting: taller waves (bigger amplitude) carry more energy; wind blows over Lake Erie for only about 240 miles, so its waves get less energy. Traps: “a big machine makes energy from nothing,” “fresh water has no energy.”
  • Numbers depend on each student’s own tests. In a sample run, Lake Erie needed about 880,000 machines (about 10,900 miles) and an Oregon buoy needed about 4,700 (about 58 miles).

Game model (for your own reference)

Power (kW) = 0.5 × height² × 8 s × machine width × fit. Widths: Small 10 m, Medium 20 m, Large 35 m. Fit: Bobbing Buoy full power at 1.5 m or taller; Flap Door full power at 2.5 m or shorter; Air Tube 75% at any height and 2 m stronger. Strength: Light 3 m, Strong 6 m, Super 12 m. One home = 1.2 kW (EIA: about 10,500 kWh a year). Ohio = about 4.9 million homes (Census). Ohio’s Lake Erie shore = about 312 miles (Ohio DNR). Every place can earn 3 stars; the stormiest places need $10+ of Buoy Detective or Buoy Training money.

Unlock codes for Classkick

Each activity shows a 🏅 certificate after real completion (for example, a CER that passes every coach check, or 2+ stars at Lake Erie and an ocean place). The certificate has a fixed unlock code students type into Classkick, plus a 3-letter name check made from the student’s name. Students screenshot the certificate into Classkick. Until then, the box shows a checklist of what is left. The code list and the name-check tool are kept in the teacher’s private planner, not on this public page.

Turning in work

Each final report has a 📤 Turn in your work box with a name field and three ways to submit:

  • 📋 Copy for Canvas: copies a formatted version (with the data table) and a plain-text version. In Canvas, students choose Text Entry, paste, and submit. If copying is blocked, a Select all box holds the same text.
  • 🖨️ Print or save as PDF: includes the data table and, for the Wave Lab, the student’s graph. Students can then upload the PDF.
  • ⬇️ Download as a text file: a .txt backup they can upload.

Set the Canvas assignment to allow Text Entry (and File Upload if you want PDFs).

Saving and starting over

Work saves automatically every 15 seconds and when the tab closes. Students can also press 💾 Save. When they come back on the same computer and browser, everything reloads. ↺ Start over asks for a second tap, then erases that activity’s work on that computer. Saved work does not move between computers, so the Canvas copy is the student’s real record.

Hosting on GitHub

  1. Unzip wave-energy.zip. The wave-energy folder has the home page (index.html), a play folder (student page), a teachers folder (this guide), the three activity files and a README.
  2. In your waves repository, choose Add file → Upload files and drag in the whole wave-energy folder. Commit.
  3. After a minute or two, open https://mslwscience.github.io/waves/wave-energy/ (the teacher-view home page). The student page is …/wave-energy/play/. Open this Teacher Guide there, and the student links above update to the real addresses.
  4. Play each activity once on a student account. Check whether the game says Live NOAA data or Saved NOAA data.

Discussion questions

  1. The water dot moves in a circle. So what does a wave actually carry to the beach?
  2. When the wave height doubled, the power became about 4 times bigger. Why might engineers care about that?
  3. Why did your machine break? What did you change, and what did it cost you?
  4. Lake Erie can’t power Ohio. Is wave power still a good idea somewhere? Where, and why?
  5. What are some problems with wave power besides wave size? (storms, ice, cost, sea life, distance to homes)
